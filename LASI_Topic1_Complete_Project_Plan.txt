Executive Summary
This document turns the selected LASI research idea into an end-to-end project that a student team can
execute in approximately two weeks. It starts from the data request and continues through data auditing,
variable construction, survey-weighted estimation, interpretation, figures, writing, and final reproducibility
checks.
The proposed design is a cross-sectional secondary analysis of LASI Wave 1 (2017-18). LASI Wave 1 is a
nationally representative ageing survey covering adults aged 45 years and above. The uploaded LASI
literature confirms that childhood socioeconomic status, childhood health, childhood bedridden experience,
depression, cognition, demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status and health variables are available in
Wave 1.

Non-negotiable interpretation rule
Because Wave 1 is a cross-sectional dataset for this project, the study estimates associations and age-group
differences. It does NOT estimate within-person cognitive decline, incidence, or causal effects over time.

The primary scientific idea is a life-course question: whether disadvantages reported for childhood are
associated with mental and cognitive outcomes measured decades later. The differentiating analytical feature
is age heterogeneity - testing whether the association is different among 45-59, 60-74 and 75+ respondents.
Recommended primary outcome: continuous LASI cognitive score. Secondary cognitive outcome: binary poor
cognition using the LASI literature-defined lower tail threshold after verifying the exact coding in the
codebook. Secondary mental-health outcome: depressive symptoms using the ten-item CES-D threshold of 4 or
more.
Recommended primary model: survey-weighted linear regression for the continuous cognitive score and
survey-weighted modified Poisson regression with robust variance for binary outcomes, with logistic
regression as a sensitivity analysis. All models must account for sampling weights, strata and PSU/cluster
identifiers.

1. Final Research Question and Scope
1.1 Primary research question
Among Indian adults aged 45 years and above, are adverse childhood socioeconomic and health conditions
associated with lower cognitive performance in later life, and does the magnitude of this association differ
across age groups?

1.2 Secondary question
Are the same childhood exposures associated with depressive symptoms, and do the age patterns look similar
for mental and cognitive outcomes?

1.3 Core hypotheses


Poorer perceived childhood socioeconomic status is associated with poorer cognitive performance.



Poorer reported childhood health is associated with poorer cognitive performance.



Having been bedridden for at least one month before age 16 is associated with poorer cognitive
performance.



The association between childhood disadvantage and cognitive/mental outcomes differs across age groups
(effect modification by age group).



Associations may attenuate after adjustment for current adult socioeconomic conditions, because
education and current economic resources may lie on the life-course pathway.

1.4 Scope boundaries


No claim of causality from Wave 1 alone.



No claim of longitudinal cognitive decline.



No merging with the 2010 pilot for the primary analysis.



No specialized biomarker, spirometry or environmental files for the core analysis.



No machine-learning prediction as the primary method; this is an epidemiological association study.

4. Technical Terms: Student-Level Definitions
Microdata: The dataset where each row is an individual/household and columns are measurements. This is
different from a factsheet, where numbers are already aggregated.
Cross-sectional study: A study where exposure and outcome are observed in the same survey round. It can
estimate associations but does not directly observe change within the same person over time.
Outcome / dependent variable: The phenomenon we are trying to explain or measure, such as cognitive
score or depressive symptoms.

LASI Wave 1 Quantitative Ageing Study - Project Plan

Exposure / independent variable: The characteristic whose association with the outcome is being estimated,
such as childhood SES.
Covariate: A variable included in a statistical model for adjustment, description or precision.
Confounder: A variable associated with both the exposure and outcome that can create a misleading
association if omitted. Adjustment is intended to reduce confounding.
Mediator: A variable on the pathway between exposure and outcome. Adjusting for a mediator can remove
part of the association being studied. Adult health may be a mediator in a life-course hypothesis.
Effect modification / interaction: The association between exposure and outcome changes depending on
another variable. Here, the main question is whether the childhood-disadvantage association differs by age
group.
Sampling weight: A number used to compensate for unequal probabilities of selection and survey
nonresponse so estimates better represent the target population.
PSU / cluster: Primary Sampling Unit or cluster used in the survey design. Responses within the same cluster
may be correlated.
Stratum: A sampling layer used by the survey design. Survey software uses strata with weights and PSU to
calculate design-correct standard errors.
Standard error (SE): A measure of statistical uncertainty around an estimated coefficient. Smaller SE
generally means a more precise estimate.
95% confidence interval (CI): A range expressing sampling uncertainty around an estimate. For ratios, a CI
crossing 1 often indicates weak evidence for an association at the conventional 5% level.
P-value: A measure used in frequentist testing. It should not be interpreted as the probability that the
hypothesis is true.
Odds ratio (OR): Ratio of odds between groups. It is not the same as a risk ratio or prevalence ratio, especially
when the outcome is common.
Prevalence ratio (PR): Ratio of outcome prevalence between exposure groups. It is often easier to interpret
than an OR for common cross-sectional outcomes.
Modified Poisson regression: A Poisson regression model with robust variance often used to estimate
prevalence ratios for binary outcomes in cross-sectional data.
Linear regression: Regression where a continuous outcome is modelled as a weighted sum of predictors plus
error; here it can model cognitive score.
Robust standard error: A standard-error estimator designed to remain valid under certain forms of variance
misspecification. In survey analysis, the full survey design should still be specified.
Multicollinearity: A situation where predictors are strongly correlated with each other, making individual
coefficients unstable. VIF is one common diagnostic.
Complete-case analysis: Analyzing respondents with nonmissing values on all variables in a model. Easy, but
it can change the study population and introduce bias if missingness is systematic.
Sensitivity analysis: A secondary analysis using a reasonable alternative definition/model to test whether the
conclusion is robust.
Predicted probability: The model-based estimated probability of an outcome for a chosen set of covariates.
Useful for interpreting interactions.

5. Conceptual Framework and Causal Language
The study is motivated by a life-course framework: conditions in childhood may be associated with
accumulated educational, socioeconomic, behavioural and health pathways that are reflected in later-life
cognition and mental health. The model is used as a conceptual guide; it does not turn a cross-sectional
association into a causal effect.

Core analytical principle
Build the primary model from variables plausibly preceding the outcome and pre-specify a second model that
adds adult socioeconomic variables. Do not call the difference between models "mediation" unless a formal
mediation analysis is conducted.

9.3 Primary continuous cognitive model
Recommended model: survey-weighted linear regression with cognitive score as the outcome. Conceptually:
CognitiveScore = beta0 + beta1(ChildhoodSES) + beta2(ChildhoodHealth) + beta3(Age) + beta4(Sex) + ... + error

Interpretation: beta for an exposure category is the adjusted difference in mean cognitive score relative to the
reference category, conditional on included covariates and the survey design.

9.4 Binary outcome models
For poor cognition and depressive symptoms, use survey-weighted modified Poisson regression with robust
variance when the outcome is common enough that odds ratios would be less intuitive. Report adjusted
prevalence ratios (APR) and 95% CIs. Run logistic regression as a sensitivity analysis and report AORs
separately.
log(E[Y]) = beta0 + beta1X1 + ... + betakXk

For a binary outcome under a modified Poisson model, exp(beta) is interpreted as an adjusted prevalence
ratio under the chosen specification.

10. Age Interaction: The Most Important Extension
Age interaction asks whether the association between childhood disadvantage and the outcome is different
for people at different ages. This is not the same as showing that a person deteriorates with age.

10.1 Specification
Create age groups 45-59, 60-74 and 75+. Fit a model containing the exposure, age-group indicators, and
exposure-by-age-group interaction terms.
Outcome = beta0 + beta1(ChildhoodSES) + beta2(AgeGroup) + beta3(ChildhoodSES x AgeGroup) + Covariates

10.2 How to test it
32. Fit the main-effects model.
33. Add the interaction terms.
34. Use a joint test of the interaction terms rather than interpreting one interaction coefficient in isolation.
35. Generate predicted probabilities or predicted mean cognitive scores for each age-by-exposure
combination.
36. Plot the estimates with 95% confidence intervals.

Example interpretation
Suppose the adjusted prevalence ratio for poor cognition associated with poor childhood SES is higher in the 75+
group than in 45-59. The appropriate statement is: "The association was stronger among adults aged 75 years and
above." Do not write "poor childhood SES caused faster cognitive decline."

11. Confounding, Mediation and Over-Adjustment
11.1 Why this matters
Students often put every available variable into one regression. That is not automatically better. Some
variables may be consequences of the exposure and therefore lie on the pathway to the outcome.

12. Survey Design: How to Get Nationally Correct Inference
LASI uses a multistage, stratified probability sampling design. Standard unweighted regression treats the
sample as if it were a simple random sample, which is not the design LASI used. The analysis should use the
provided survey weights and design identifiers.

16. How to Interpret the Numbers
16.1 Linear regression example
Suppose beta = -1.20 for poor childhood SES. The interpretation would be: respondents in the poor childhoodSES category had, on average, 1.20 points lower cognitive score than the reference group, conditional on the
variables in the model. This is an association, not a causal effect.

16.2 Prevalence ratio example
Suppose APR = 1.25 for poor childhood health. The interpretation would be: the prevalence of the binary
outcome was estimated to be about 25% higher in the poor-childhood-health group than in the reference
group, conditional on the model covariates and survey design.

16.3 Confidence interval example
Suppose APR = 1.25 with 95% CI 1.10-1.42. The estimate suggests a positive association and the interval
excludes 1.00. Report the estimate and uncertainty, not only the p-value.

16.4 Non-significant interaction
If the interaction test is not statistically persuasive, do not manufacture separate "effects by age" conclusions.
Report that strong evidence of heterogeneity by age was not detected, while still presenting descriptive
patterns if informative.

23. What If Nothing Is Significant?
A null result is not a failed project. A rigorous study can find weak or absent associations. The correct
response is to report the magnitude, confidence interval and plausible explanations: measurement error,
retrospective reporting of childhood conditions, residual confounding, heterogeneity across cohorts, selective
survival, or insufficient variation in the exposure. Do not "search" for a significant subgroup until one
appears.

24. Minimum Viable Project (MVP) if Time Runs Out
The 2-day emergency version
If the team loses time, keep only: (1) one childhood exposure - childhood SES; (2) primary outcome - continuous
cognitive score; (3) age groups 45-59 / 60-74 / 75+; (4) age interaction; (5) core demographic + current SES
adjustment; (6) survey-weighted linear regression; (7) one predicted-value figure. Add depression and sensitivity
analyses only after this core analysis is complete.
